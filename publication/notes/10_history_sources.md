# 10 · History and folklore: source audit

This note records what the history page claims and how firm each claim is. It was built from three research passes (September 2026), with the key facts re-checked against the sources themselves. Numbered references match `docs/history.html`.

## Findings that change the usual story

- **Ellison's 1883 sighting is family tradition, not a contemporary record.**
  - Ellison's 1937 memoir does not mention the lights (his granddaughter Rosemary Cox, in Hall, *Texas Monthly* 2006) [6].
  - The sighting is stated as fact on THC marker No. 3208 (1988) [11] and in the Handbook of Texas [1].
- **Earliest written story:** *San Angelo Times*, February 1945, "Ghost Light Appears in Marfa Area" [6]. This is earlier than the 1957 *Coronet* piece that is often called the first published account [2].
- **Early tales describe one light**, called the "Ghost Light", "Smuggler's Light" or "Alsate's Light" [6].
- **Alsate's Ghost** reached print at third hand: Clayton Williams, from his grandfather O. W. Williams, from the guide Juan Cano, from "the Indians" [6]. Alsate himself is historical [4]. A separate legend names the Chisos Mountains after his ghost [29].
- **An 1883 debunking is reported:** a Southern Pacific survey engineer triangulated the lights to kerosene lanterns on the Jesús Rojas ranch. The only source is Cartwright, *Texas Monthly* 1984, with no citation [10].
- **The Handbook of Texas has an error:** it names "Midland Army Air Field outside Marfa". The base on the flat was Marfa Army Air Field [1, 3].
- **Marfa Army Air Field closing date conflicts:** the Handbook says 31 December 1945; the 2007 THC marker says September 1945.
- **The festival's first year is uncertain.** Texas Monthly gives 1986 [6], but the 37th festival in 2024 implies 1988 [5].
- **Viewing Center:**
  - It began as a 1998 class project by Marfa High School teacher Felicia Locke [13].
  - It opened in 2003 at a cost of $720,000, from federal and TxDOT funds [6, 1].

## Claims we could not verify (kept off the page or flagged)

- Hallie Stillwell's 1916 sighting. It appears only on a fan wiki and a personal site. It is absent from her Handbook entry, Wikipedia and her *Texas Monthly* obituary piece [31].
- The Apache "fallen stars" belief. It appears on the Viewing Center marker [12], but no ethnographic source was found. No documented Indigenous tradition about the lights was found at all.
- An early source for the "conquistadors seeking gold" story. Dobie records lights marking buried treasure in South Texas and New Mexico, not the Big Bend [32].
- Fritz Kahl as a 1943 pilot witness.
- The author and title of the *Coronet* article (Wikipedia gives Paul Moran, "The Mystery of the Texas Ghost Light", *Coronet* 42(3)). Not independently confirmed.
- Brueske's first edition: 1988 or 1989.
- A Texas Folklore Society article on the lights. None was found in eight volumes checked. Elton Miles, *Tales of the Big Bend* (TAMU Press), is the key folklore source still to be read.

## Comparable cases (for the manuscript's discussion)

- **Brown Mountain, North Carolina** (USGS Circular 646) [25].
  - Sterrett (1913): locomotive headlights.
  - Mansfield (1922): of the lights he studied instrumentally, 47% were car headlights, 33% locomotive headlights, 10% stationary lights and 10% brush fires.
  - Mansfield triangulated from known stations, the same method as our ray fan.
- **Paulding Light, Michigan** (Michigan Tech, 2010) [26]: headlights on US-45, about 4.5 mi away. Every appearance matched a passing car. Reproduced with hazard lights.
- **Min Min light, Queensland** (Pettigrew 2003) [27]: an inverted mirage (Fata Morgana). Reproduced with a car about 10 km away, below the line of sight, seen by six observers (ABC Science).
- **Hessdalen, Norway** [28]:
  - Project Hessdalen started in 1983, with a field campaign in 1984 (53 events recorded) and an automatic station since 1998.
  - The energy source "remains completely unknown" (Caron & Faridi 2016).
  - This is the benchmark for what establishing a genuine anomaly requires.
- **Hornet Spooklight, Missouri/Oklahoma:** attributed to Route 66 headlights, with timed blink tests. Only a secondary source (Route 66 News, 2018) was found, so it was not used on the page.

## For the manuscript

The history section should present the lights as a case where the oral tradition is older than the written record. The earliest accounts are thin (no bearing, time or duration), which is why neither "just headlights" nor "unexplained" can be settled from history alone. The field protocol exists to fill that gap.
