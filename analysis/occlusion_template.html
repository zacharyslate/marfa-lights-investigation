Where US‑67 can be seen from the Viewing Area

A 0.7 m headlight on US‑67 between Shafter and Marfa, tested for terrain occlusion from an eye 1.6 m above the Marfa Lights Viewing Area. Earth curvature and atmospheric refraction are included. Terrain comes from the USGS 3DEP elevation model at about 30 m resolution.

Across refraction states

Colour is the smallest refraction coefficient k at which a headlight becomes visible. Night surface inversions raise k; daytime heating lowers it.

    Road that stays visible at this k is drawn at full strength. The temperature gradient uses k = 503·P/T²·(0.0343 + dT/dz) at P ≈ 850 hPa, T ≈ 283 K (Hirt et al. 2010).

    How it was computed

    • US‑67 comes from your Google Earth trace, resampled every 60 m, with 1,077 points from Shafter (km 0) to Marfa (km 64.6).
    • For each point, terrain is sampled every 15 m along the great-circle path from the viewer using bilinear DEM interpolation.
    • Apparent heights drop by d²(1 − k)/2R. The visibility threshold kcrit is solved in closed form, and a spot-check against brute force at ±0.01 found no mismatches.
    • The first 30 m and last 60 m of each path are skipped so the viewer's own pad and the car's own road bed don't count as obstacles.

    What limits it

    • Constant k. Near-ground gradients vary strongly with height. Hirt et al. measured k from about −4 by day to +16 near sunset at 1.8 m. A ray-trace through a layered temperature profile is the next step for the grazing paths.
    • Humidity barely bends visible light. The water-vapour term in the optical refractive index is small (Ciddor 1996). Humidity matters here mainly through haze and extinction, which dims lights. It does not change what is occluded.
    • 30 m terrain. This resolution misses road cuts, embankments, fences, brush and buildings. "Marginal" flags where that matters.
    • Headlights are directional. This model says where a lamp is geometrically visible, not whether it points at you. Road heading is the next layer.
